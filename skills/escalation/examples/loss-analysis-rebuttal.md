audience: peer exec

## Situation

A product marketing manager in another team reports that product causes <n>% of lost ARR to a competitor.
Yu-Kuan thinks the number is wrong. He first asks for a joint analysis.
Later he sends a short cover note with a rebuttal memo attached.

## Sent

### A. <date> (after the first plan)

`````text
Hi @<PMM>  I also have @<analyst> working on the deeply on the win-loss analysis vs <competitor>. can I ask you @<PMM> to please work together w/ <analyst> on the data analysis, so we have the same picture?

I have a lot of questions on how we arrived at the “product being cited for <n>% of loss reasons” to <competitor>, but I’ll hold my questions till he & you can align on the data and readout on root causes?

YL
`````

### B. <date> (rebuttal memo attached)

`````text
Hi <PMM>,

Thanks for all your work on this, I reviewed your reporting &  discussed w/ <peer exec>  - I think we should be very careful about what headlines and conclusions we draw, as opposed to just doing a data readout. E.g. some of the conclusions may change a lot if we actually dig into customer commentary, conversations or even change the time period. Here’s a deeper analysis, let’s discuss more in the meeting?

YL
`````

## Why it works

- The first note sends the work to two analysts and holds his questions. He does not argue the numbers in the open yet.
- He puts the claim in quotes ("product being cited for <n>% of loss reasons") to show doubt without an attack.
- The rebuttal note is four sentences. The evidence goes in the attachment, not the body.
- He criticises the method, not the person: "be very careful about what headlines and conclusions we draw".
- He names the colleague he checked with, so he is not alone.
- He closes with a meeting ask, not a verdict.
