# Insight patterns — the analytical moves that make a win/loss report worth reading

These are the repeatable *moves* that separated the July 2026 "golden" reports from a
report that only restates numbers. A mediocre report tells you the win rate. A good one
tells you why the win rate is *the wrong number to look at*, what it hides, and what
actually moved the deals. Apply these on any period — the July examples are illustrative,
not the point.

Each pattern: the reusable move, the July worked example, and the trigger
("apply this whenever…"). The through-line under all of them: **read every headline number
at record level, name the mechanism, and falsify the obvious story before you print it.**

**These are candidates, not a checklist.** A period has two or three real stories. Run every
pattern as a TEST during STEP 3b (region synthesis) and STEP 3d (portfolio synthesis), then
publish only the patterns that changed what the report says. A pattern that confirms the
ordinary result is a line in the demoted list, not a section. Applying all fifteen across nine
cells produces a complete report that nobody can use.

---

## 0. Name the decision attribute, or it is not an insight — RUN THIS FIRST, ON EVERY INSIGHT

**The move.** Build this chain for every candidate insight:
`context or trigger → decision attribute or friction → Acme intervention → buyer-behavior
change → outcome`. The root cause is the **decision attribute** and its effect on buyer
behavior — an observable property that separates comparable wins from losses. The intervention
is the lever we control. Do NOT merge the two: "change the target list" is a lever; "public
company transition inside six months" is the attribute. Every rung carries its own citation.

**Banned terminal labels.** A candidate that stops at any of these FAILS the gate — they name a
cohort, a channel, or a lever we own, never the attribute that changed the decision:
`referral` · `target list` · `expansion` · `installed base` · `qualification` · `channel` ·
`new logo` · `coverage` · `package` · `price` · `team` · 'carries a soft code' · 'dies
pre-Proposal' · 'Unresponsive' · 'no economic buyer' · 'no decision' · 'budget' · 'the field is
lossy' · 'brand strength' · 'the customer went quiet'. These are the codes a rep typed or the
levers we hold. They are not causes.

A candidate passes only when it has a within-cohort win/loss contrast, at least three named
proof deals, one contradictory case, and cited decision evidence. If any element is missing,
mark it `DESCRIPTIVE — NOT A ROOT CAUSE` and demote it before ARR ranking.

**Disaggregate before you demote (win side and loss side).** A broad capability (product, AI,
security, referral) that appears on BOTH wins and losses is NOT thereby a non-cause. Subdivide it
until you find the specific observable sub-attribute that separates the comparable wins from the
comparable losses, and publish THAT. Worked example: "product" appears on wins and losses;
subdivide to "Acme One Platform sold as ONE consolidated licence" — it wins at 46.4% against
10.4% for non-platform deals, and the one platform LOSS (AG2R La Mondiale) happened only when the
same platform was pitched as two separate products. Mark a candidate `DESCRIPTIVE` ONLY after a
genuine subdivision attempt finds no separating sub-attribute. AI stays table stakes unless a
separating sub-attribute (a governed, approved place for board data — risk containment, not
feature superiority) is found; never claim AI as a net product win.

**The chain notation is analyst-internal ONLY.** The `context → attribute → intervention → change
→ outcome` chain is a reasoning tool for you, the analyst. It must NEVER appear in report output.
In the report body and every table cell, write the mechanism as two or three short plain sentences
in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English — no `→` arrows, no clause-joining `;` semicolons.
Model cell: "A director on the buying board already runs Acme elsewhere. That removes the
board's adoption risk, so the buyer chooses Acme. Proof: Viper Energy, Fossil, Zura Bio."

**Worked shape** (finish it from the data; do not publish the chain below as a conclusion):
loss coded 'Unresponsive' → the named contact was not the buyer (Central States: a junior
admin; Mitsui: no board meetings) → the attribute separating these losses from comparable wins
is **no board-meeting cadence at the account** (present in the losses, absent in the wins) →
our qualification gate does not test for it → **the gate is the intervention; the missing
board-cadence attribute is the cause**.

Where a rung cannot be evidenced, stop at the last evidenced rung, name the missing data as
Open Data Request #1, and record it in the appendix — never in the body. The attribute-level
cause goes in the `Causal mechanism + proof deals` column of the Key Insights table and drives
the matching Strategic Implications row.

**Apply whenever** you write any insight, at any level. This pattern is not optional and it is
not a candidate — every other pattern below feeds it.

## 1. Sub-region can beat segment as the explanatory variable

**The move.** Before you organise a region's story around segment (ENT/MM/SMB), test
whether geography-*within*-region explains more of the outcome. Split the region by
country or sub-region and compare the spread. If one sub-region carries most of the ARR,
most of the wins, or a wildly different rate, lead with geography and say the segment label
explains less than "where the deal sits."

**July example.** EMEA: UKI carried ~64% of won ARR ($333K of $514K) and 9 of 14 Enterprise
wins; all 14 EMEA Enterprise wins were Referral-sourced. "Enterprise" explained far less than
"UKI installed base + Upsell type." On the loss side, EMEA got its own "sub-region beats
segment" page: UKI 68 / DACH 26 / FR&BE 15 / ME&A 12 losses, *each losing for a different
reason*. A segment-only read would have blended these into mush. AMER got the same treatment
one level down: Canada 16.0% vs US 27.5% win rate, same *mechanism* but Canada materially worse.

**Apply whenever** a region has an uneven geographic footprint, or when the segment cuts look
similar but you suspect the region isn't homogeneous. Always run the sub-region split as a
candidate explanatory variable; publish it when its spread beats the segment spread.

## 2. The win/loss is not what the label says — reclassify at record level

**The move.** The reason-code picklist and the pipeline stage are *claims*, not facts. Read
each material deal at record level and reclassify. Three recurring corrections:
- **Expansion miscoded as new-logo** — an upsell/renewal/cross-sell to an existing customer
  logged in new-business pipeline. Always separate net-new from expansion and recompute the
  real net-new denominator.
- **Internal-product move miscoded as competitive loss** — the "competitor" is a
  Acme-owned product (Brainloop, BoardEffect, GovernAI). The ARR often stays inside the
  house, so a "loss" overstates BU-level revenue loss.
- **A no-bid or non-deal coded as "Chose Competitor"** — we withdrew, it was a data-residency
  walkaway, it was an RFI for a different product, or there was no board intent at all.

**July example.** AMER MM: 14 of 45 "losses" already ran Acme Boards (failed cross-sells
miscoded into BLC pipeline) → true net-new denominator ~31, not 45. DACH's largest loss,
Swisscom $45,783, went to Brainloop — a Acme-owned product; reported plainly as internal
competition. APAC: *both* "Chose Competitor" rows were mislabels — Dah Sing was a Acme
no-bid over Hong Kong data residency; Racing Victoria was a failed GovernAI trial. True APAC
head-to-head competitive losses: 0 of 72.

**Apply whenever** you cite a rate, a "competitive" count, or a named-competitor loss. Never
take a reason code or a "new business" tag at face value on a deal that matters. Always ask:
is this net-new or expansion? Is this "competitor" one of ours?

## 3. The amendment / small-deal inflation move — compute both bases, lead with the delta

**The move.** A headline rate often rests on a pile of low-value contract tweaks that carry
no competitive signal. Compute the rate on *both* a raw basis and a cleaned basis that strips
sub-threshold amendments (and dedup rows). The gap between the two is itself the finding —
lead with it, don't bury it. Never publish a rate without naming its filter chain.

**July example.** The `< $5K Amendment` filter removed 8% of losses but **41% of wins** — 62
of 150 raw wins were sub-$5K upsell amendments totalling $101,237. Headline win rate 28.63%
all-sizes collapsed to 20.37% Finance-clean. APAC looked like the strongest region at 42.9%
until you saw 31 of 54 raw wins were amendments → 26.7% clean, in line with everyone else.
The reports stated the asymmetry in the "how to read this" section of *both* documents and
carried both columns everywhere.

**Apply whenever** a rate looks unusually strong/weak, or the numerator is padded with small
transactions. Always publish two bases side by side and make the delta a stated finding, not a
footnote. Flag the exact filter (and whose control cell sets it) as an open data request if
the definition is uncertain.

## 4. Installed-base farming vs conquest — quantify the split

**The move.** Establish the cross-report through-line: are we winning/losing on
installed-base gravity (expansion + referral + director familiarity) or on head-to-head
conquest against rivals? Quantify the split explicitly, then hold every regional finding
against it. The *ratio* is the strategic message.

**July example.** Win report through-line: 53 of 88 clean wins (60%) were expansion of
existing accounts; 52 of 88 (59%) referral-sourced; named-competitor takeaways only ~6 of 88.
All 14 EMEA Enterprise wins Referral; all 5 APAC Enterprise wins AcmeOne upsells; SMB was
the actual new-logo engine (27 of 35 clean new logos). Loss report mirror image: genuine
competitive defeat was narrow (SMB price in AMER; Sherpany in DACH), most "losses" were
CRM-hygiene, internal-Brainloop, or coverage failures. "We win where we already are; we rarely
lose head-to-head because we rarely fight head-to-head."

**Apply whenever** you write the executive summary or strategic implications. Always land the
conquest-vs-farming ratio as a number, and make sure it is consistent across the win and loss
reports (they should tell two halves of one story).

## 5. The outlier earns a mechanism chain — a dedicated section only if it is not already an insight

**The move.** An abnormal cell (a 2% win rate in a sea of 20-30%) is the single most valuable
finding in the report. Give it a dedicated section **where its mechanism is not already one of
that region's top-three insights**; where the insight already carries the mechanism, absorb the
outlier into that insight as two sentences naming the cell and its rate, and write no separate
section (SKILL.md STEP 4). Either way, do not just report the number — walk the
*mechanism chain*: source → stage-of-death → packaging → what that adds up to. Distinguish a
go-to-market defect from a product defeat.

**July example.** AMER Mid-Market got its own page in both reports. 1 real win against 44
losses (2%). Mechanism chain: 67% outbound-sourced → 60% die at Discovery → 87% never had a
SKU entered → the one win (Lyntris) came via an NYSE listing-subsidy referral, not a contest;
MM Outbound 0W/41L worldwide; the packaging ladder is priced on private-vs-public not size, so
no tier is aimed at a mid-market company. Verdict: a coverage-and-packaging failure, not a
price defeat — a battlecard would not move it, a fitted SKU and tighter qualification would.

**Apply whenever** any cell is a statistical outlier (very high or very low vs its neighbours).
Always give it a causal chain that a reader could act on, and name the fixable lever — as a
standalone section if the mechanism is new, or inside the region insight if it is not.

## 6. Falsify your own hypotheses — in the RESEARCH file, not in the report body

**The move.** Every strong claim is tested against the competing explanations, with the evidence,
in the research file. A claim that was never tested gets discounted. **The report publishes the
surviving conclusion alone** — the rejected alternatives and their evidence go to the appendix or
the companion appendix file. "tested and rejected", "tested and ruled out", "falsified" and
"counter-hypothesis" are banned from body real estate: a report that argues with itself in front
of the reader spends pages defending a finding instead of stating it.

**July example.** Counter-tests recorded in the research files as failed:
- "Stage-gating explains APAC's zero competitive coding" — refuted: APAC's early-loss share
  (66.7%) is *lower* than AMER's (72.5%), yet it codes 0 of 23 late-stage losses competitive
  vs EMEA's 11 of 38. So it's a coding-discipline gap, not a stage gap.
- "MM fails differently on the loss side" — refuted: MM early-loss share 68.2% ≈ ENT 68.4% ≈
  SMB 69.6%; the short MM medians were *win* medians (MM losses actually run longer).
- "BoardWise is taking DACH" — refuted: never the confirmed winner of a single mined deal.
- "A single ME&A vendor is beating us" — refuted: no external vendor named as winner of any
  ME&A loss.

**Apply whenever** you make a causal claim strong enough to drive a decision. Write the
counter-test and its result in the research file and the appendix. If you didn't try to break the
claim, you haven't earned it — and if you print the attempt in the body, you have spent the
reader's attention on your own doubt.

## 7. Catch fabricated / derived stats masquerading as measured

**The move.** Interrogate any suspiciously precise or convenient statistic for provenance. A
number pulled off a chart gridline, back-derived from a total, or produced by a model is not a
measurement — say so and refute it. Check arithmetic on inherited figures; if the categories
don't sum or a stated average doesn't divide, the source figure is suspect.

**July example.** The prior report's "22.5% of Enterprise accounts publishing zero board books"
was refuted as a **chart y-axis gridline in $M**, not a statistic. Lost ARR was flagged as
visibly *modelled* (only 42 distinct values across 72 APAC rows; $6,679.34852 appears 11 times)
— never presented as a negotiated price. The prior "19 contested" figure was shown to be
unreproducible, and the report it came from failed its own arithmetic (categories summed to 48
not 47; $1.74M/47 = $37.0K not the claimed $96.5K; six named deals consumed $149.4K of a
claimed $157K). And when July's own contested test *also* returned 19, that coincidence was
flagged explicitly so no one would read it as vindication.

**Apply whenever** you inherit a figure from a prior deck, or a number is oddly round/precise/
convenient. Always trace it to a measured source; if it's derived or modelled, label it and
refuse to let it stand as measured. Re-add the components and check they tie.

## 8. Coding-discipline gaps are findings, not gaps to paper over

**The move.** When a field is blank, wrong, or uniformly empty, that *absence* is a reportable
finding — plus a specific, owned, answerable open-data-request. Do not silently work around it
and do not fabricate a value to fill it. State what the absence means for what the report can
and cannot claim.

**July example.** The competitor-name field
(`OPP_COMPETITOR_PROSPECT_CURRENTLY_USES`) was `No SFDC Data` on 434 of 434 raw July losses —
so *no* named-competitor count comes from Finance data; every rival name is flagged as
free-text/Gong-sourced and "anecdotal." The win-reason field was blank on 54% of AMER wins, so
themes were ranked by sourced evidence, not picklist frequency — and "Why is win-reason blank
on 54% of AMER wins?" became a numbered open data request with an owner (AMER Sales Ops). The
reports stated the picklist is unreliable *in both directions* (over-attributes competitors
where none existed; hides real competitive losses under "no interest").

**Apply whenever** a field you wanted to use is empty/inconsistent/wrong. Always convert the
gap into (a) a stated limit on the report's claims and (b) a numbered open-data-request with a
suggested owner. Never paper over it or invent the missing value.

## 9. Say what a grouping hides

**The move.** Every aggregation conceals something. When you fold categories together, name
what the fold hides — especially if a folded-in sub-group is the worst (or best) performer.
Flag when a source table is silently filtered or when a coverage tier is being treated as a
size band.

**July example.** Folding Strategic into Enterprise (which is *not* Finance's own convention)
hid that **Strategic is the worst-retaining segment at 89.26%** — invisible in the blended
Enterprise number. The reports said so and flagged that Strategic is a coverage tier, not a
size band. Separately: the workbook's `GDR` sheet was saved *filtered to Segment=Enterprise*,
so its "91.9% total" was an Enterprise number mislabelled as BLC — flagged, not repeated. And
"Downsell" was shown to be misnamed: 6 rows ($602,521, 63% of downsell ARR) were *total* Boards
loss filed as downsell.

**Apply whenever** you aggregate, fold, or inherit a pre-aggregated table. Always state what the
grouping conceals, whether a source table is silently filtered/mislabelled, and whether a label
(segment, region, "downsell") actually means what it says.

## Win-side patterns (10–14) — the moves the loss-heavy examples above under-teach

Patterns 1–9 lean on loss examples. The failure mode they do *not* guard against is a win
report that ships a flattering, un-falsified story — "we win on AI", "we win on brand" —
that nobody stress-tested. The five below are the win-side counterparts: same discipline
(read at record level, name the mechanism, falsify before you print), aimed at the specific
ways a *winning* narrative fools you. Two of them (12, 14) are the win-cohort
operationalisation of the cross-report moves in #4 and #6 — run per cohort, not just once.

---

## 10. "AI is the trigger, not the purchase" — test what the customer actually paid for

**The move.** When "AI" shows up as a win reason, test whether the customer actually *paid*
for AI or paid for something else that AI merely triggered. AI is often the "why now" that
opens or unlocks an upgrade while the buyer declines the paid AI add-on itself. Report AI as
a catalyst, not the line item — unless the money says otherwise.

**July example.** Swinburne named AI-strategy alignment as a buying driver but declined the
shipped GovernAI add-on on price; what she actually bought was uncapped AcmeOne
licensing — "it takes the brakes off." Across the report GovernAI recurs as the Enterprise
upgrade trigger (Newcastle, Dulux, Dyno Nobel), yet buyers pay for the platform/licensing,
not the AI line. In AMER SMB, "GovernAI as a buying driver" was seller-pushed, not
customer-pulled (see #14).

**Apply whenever** "AI" appears as a win reason. Never print "we win on AI" without checking
the invoice: did they pay for the AI SKU, or for the platform/seats/licensing that AI merely
gave them a reason to buy *now*? Name which, every time.

---

## 11. Decode the win-reason picklist into the real mechanism

**The move.** The win-reason picklist label hides the true driver — translate it, never
restate it. "Brand Strength" almost always means the **boardroom director network** (a
director already runs Acme on another board and pulls it in), not abstract reputation.
"Referral" means a **warm existing relationship**, not a marketing-channel lead. Verify the
decoded mechanism in a call or a deal note before you print it.

**July example.** AMER "Brand Strength" decoded to director familiarity / SSO across seats
(VICI, Jones, Csquare) — Csquare's CAO: "I have been a board member in another company and
acme was our platform." APAC "Security" at BlueCHP decoded to an AI-search-roadmap win
over BoardEffect; NZ Bioeconomy "Price/Value" decoded to merger + incumbency; Hamilton /
Toubani "Brand Strength" decoded to prior-employer familiarity. All 14 EMEA Enterprise
"Referral" wins decoded to warm existing relationships, not a partner channel.

**Apply whenever** you cite a win reason. Never restate a picklist label verbatim; state the
underlying mechanism and cite the call/note that confirms it. If you can't verify the
mechanism, say the label is unverified — don't launder it into a finding.

---

## 12. Separate expansion from conquest on the win side too — compute the split per cohort

**The move.** Most "wins" are upsell / renewal / cross-sell into accounts that already own
the product; only a small minority are true competitive new-logo takeaways. Before you call
any win cohort "competitively strong," compute (a) the expansion-vs-new-logo split and (b)
the named-competitor-takeaway share. This is the per-cohort operationalisation of #4's
cross-report through-line — run it on *every* cell you want to praise, not just once.

**July example.** ~53 of 88 clean wins (60%) were expansion; named-competitor takeaways were
only ~6 of 88. All 5 APAC Enterprise wins were AcmeOne upsells (zero net-new logos); 13
of 14 EMEA Enterprise wins were referral-sourced expansion (one net-new, ICG). The only APAC
head-to-head all month (BlueCHP vs BoardEffect) was against a Acme-*owned* product — so
APAC's apparent competitive strength was zero real conquest once decoded.

**Apply whenever** you're tempted to call a cohort competitively strong. Compute the split
first: a high win count built on expansion is not evidence you beat rivals, and a
"takeaway" against a house-owned product is not a conquest.

## 13. Referral-conversion as the durable edge — and its amendment trap

**The move.** A high referral-conversion rate can be the *real, durable* win driver — but
check it isn't inflated by the same sub-threshold amendments that pattern 3 strips, before
you celebrate it. Report referral conversion on the clean basis and decompose the excluded
rows; the raw-vs-clean gap is itself the finding.

**July example.** APAC referral converted at 67.9% vs AMER 44.3% / EMEA 46.6% — a genuine
edge. But 26 of the 31 excluded sub-$5K amendments (84%) were referral-sourced, so the raw
referral lift overstated the clean-basis reality. Among the 23 *real* APAC wins, referral is
10 (43%), tied with outbound (10); marketing is 3. The durable edge survives the correction,
but only after you decompose it.

**Apply whenever** referral (or any lead source) conversion looks like the headline win
driver. Report it on the clean basis and decompose the excluded amendments before calling it
durable — a source rate padded with paperwork is not an edge.

---

## 14. Falsify win claims too — name the alternative you tested and rejected

**The move.** The falsified-hypothesis discipline (#6) applies symmetrically: every headline
*win* driver states the competing explanation you tested and *rejected*, with evidence. A win
report that only confirms its flattering thesis gets discounted exactly like a loss report
that does.

**July example.** "GovernAI is an SMB buying driver" was **refuted** — in the AMER SMB
lower-ARR cohort it was a seller-led attach, not a customer pull; the only genuine customer
AI-pull was Hill's Resort (already using AI for minutes) and Relm (action-tracker pain).
Likewise the single AMER Mid-Market "win" (Lyntris) was tested as a product win and
rejected: it was an NYSE listing-subsidy referral with no incumbent displaced and no
feature bake-off — which is why cold MM outbound went 0-for-41 worldwide.

**Apply whenever** you print a headline win driver strong enough to steer a decision. Name
the counter-explanation you tested and its result inline. If you didn't try to break the win
story, you haven't earned it.

---

## 15. Credit the product win the evidence supports — cautiously optimistic, not grudging

**The move.** Patterns 10-14 falsify a flattering story; this one guards the opposite failure —
a win report so busy debunking itself that it never credits a real product win. When a metric or
a customer quote shows the product genuinely winning, state it as a win and attach the caveat in
the same breath, not the reverse. Report the uncomfortable finding plainly, but do not weight a
well-sourced success under a pile of hedges.

**Illustration.** The Acme One platform win-rate lift (46.4% clean vs 10.4%), governed AI as
a security pull a customer acted on (Newcastle), and a data-residency capability that closed a
regulated India deal (Kotak) are real, evidence-backed product strengths — lead with them, then
note the association-not-causation caveat or the shipped-vs-planned gap. "We win by installed-base
farming" is a durability strength, not a confession.

**Apply whenever** you draft a win narrative or Strategic Implications. Land the product's real
wins with the same confidence you land its gaps. Optimism is earned from the evidence, never
invented — no exclusivity claims, no unevidenced superiority.

## Cross-cutting habits that show up in every pattern above

- **Name the mechanism, always.** Every theme table has a "Mechanism (the why)" column. A
  theme without a mechanism is just a label. The "why" is the deliverable.
- **Basis discipline is non-negotiable.** No rate, count, or ASP appears without its filter
  chain. Two bases, both published, delta stated.
- **Source discipline.** Anecdotal (free-text/call) evidence is labelled anecdotal;
  measured Finance figures are labelled measured; a genuinely derived figure is labelled
  modelled, with its derivation named on the same line. **A direct sum is never labelled
  modelled** — lost opportunity ARR is a `REPORTING_ARR` sum, so it reads "not booked revenue".
  Never cite a report as its own source.
- **Report the uncomfortable finding plainly, without editorialising** about strategy or
  another team's performance. Put the counter-evidence in; a report that only flatters gets
  discounted.
- **Every gap becomes a numbered, owned, answerable open-data-request** — the report ends by
  telling the reader exactly what would change what it can say, and who can answer it.
